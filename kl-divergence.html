Part 1

Build the definition yourself

🔒 Locked — finish the warm-up above first.

Setup: data really comes from a distribution P (the truth), but you only have a model Q. We'll derive KL from the idea of surprise.

Surprise of an outcome x under a distribution is "how unlikely did I think this was": rare events are very surprising, certain events not at all, and surprise of two independent events should add. The function with these properties is the negative log of the probability.

Recall 1.1You believe Q, and outcome x occurs. Write the formula for your surprise. Then: an all-knowing observer who believes the true P sees the same x. Write their surprise. Finally write your excess surprise (yours minus theirs) — simplify it into a single log.

Reveal unlocks after a genuine attempt (40+ characters).

Answer

Your surprise: −log Q(x)   Their surprise: −log P(x)

Excess surprise: −log Q(x) − (−log P(x)) = log [ P(x) / Q(x) ]

This single quantity — the log-ratio — is the atom of KL divergence. It's positive when the truth assigned more probability than your model did (you were too surprised), negative when your model over-predicted x.

Recall 1.2KL divergence is the average excess surprise. But averaged over what — outcomes drawn from P, or outcomes drawn from Q? Pick one and justify it before revealing. (Hint: who generates the actual data in this story?)

Reveal unlocks after a genuine attempt (40+ characters).

Answer

In this story, reality (P) generates the data, so we average over x ~ P:

KL(P ∥ Q) = Ex~P [ log P(x)/Q(x) ] = Σx P(x) log [P(x)/Q(x)]

This is the forward KL. But notice: nothing stops you from averaging the other log-ratio under Q instead:

KL(Q ∥ P) = Ex~Q [ log Q(x)/P(x) ]

That's the reverse KL. Two changes happen at once when you flip: which distribution you sample from and which is on top of the ratio. Memorize the pattern: the first argument is both the sampler and the numerator.

(Units: log base e → "nats"; log base 2 → "bits". We'll use nats.)

Check 1.3One of these properties does not hold for KL divergence. Which?

Correct

KL is not symmetric — and that asymmetry is not a technical nuisance, it's the entire subject of this course. KL(P ∥ Q) and KL(Q ∥ P) measure genuinely different things and produce dramatically different behavior when minimized. You're about to feel that with your own hands.

Recall 2.3Different numbers — same two distributions. Look at your forward-KL computation: which single term made it large, and what does that term mean in words? (Which probability did Q get badly wrong, as judged by P?)

Reveal unlocks after a genuine attempt (40+ characters).

Answer

The term 0.5·ln(0.5/0.1) = 0.80 dominates. In words: reality puts a full 50% on tails, but Q only allows 10% — and forward KL weights that error by how often reality actually produces tails (50% of the time).

Forward KL is the truth grading your model: errors in places P visits often get amplified; errors in places P never visits get weight P(x) = 0 and vanish entirely. Hold that thought.

Now push it to the extreme. Suppose your model goes all-in: Q = (1, 0) — it says tails is impossible. P is still (0.5, 0.5).

Check 2.4KL(P ∥ Q) is now…

Correct

The tails term is 0.5·ln(0.5/0) = +∞. Forward KL hands out an infinite penalty whenever Q assigns zero to anything P can actually produce. A model minimizing forward KL will therefore never dare put zero (or near-zero) where P has mass — it must cover everything P does. This is called being zero-avoiding.

Check 2.5Same Q = (1, 0), but now the reverse direction: KL(Q ∥ P) = ?

Correct

Only the heads term survives: 1·ln(1/0.5) = ln 2 ≈ 0.69 — a small, finite penalty. The tails term has weight Q(tails) = 0, so it simply doesn't count.

Sit with the contrast: the same pair of distributions costs ∞ one way and 0.69 the other. Reverse KL doesn't care that Q abandoned half of reality — because Q is the sampler, and Q never goes there to be judged. Reverse KL only punishes Q for placing mass where P is small (try Q = (0.5, 0.5) vs P = (1,0) mentally: now reverse KL is the infinite one).

You now have both halves of the central intuition. Time to watch it play out geometrically.

Recall 3.1Explain, in terms of which distribution the expectation samples from, why each challenge ended where it did. Why did one solution cover and the other commit to a single bump?

Reveal unlocks after a genuine attempt (80+ characters).

Answer

Forward, KL(P ∥ Q) = EP[log P/Q]: samples come from P, i.e. from both bumps. Any bump where Q is tiny makes log(P/Q) explode — the Part 2 infinity in soft form. The only way a single Gaussian can keep Q non-negligible at both bumps is to sit between them and widen. It accepts wasting mass on the valley (where P ≈ 0) because forward KL never samples the valley, so that waste is barely charged. Result: mass-covering / mean-seeking / zero-avoiding. (Fun fact: for a Gaussian family, the exact forward-KL optimum is moment matching — Q takes P's true mean and variance.)

Reverse, KL(Q ∥ P) = EQ[log Q/P]: samples come from Q itself. Mass Q puts in the valley gets judged by log(Q/P) with P ≈ 0 there — ruinous. So Q retreats until everywhere it stands, P is large: snugly onto one bump. The other bump? Q never samples it, so abandoning it is free. Result: mode-seeking / zero-forcing — and note there are two equally good optima (either bump); reverse KL is happy with either and your optimizer's starting point decides.

Part 4

The mechanics, nailed down

🔒 Locked — complete both lab challenges.

You've seen the behavior. Now make the mechanism so explicit you could re-derive it cold in six months. No formulas revealed until you produce them.

Recall 4.1Write the forward KL integral. Then explain precisely why minimizing it makes Q "zero-avoiding": which regions of x control the integral, what happens to the integrand where P > 0 but Q → 0, and what happens where Q > 0 but P → 0?

This one needs a real answer — reveal unlocks at 100 characters.

Answer

KL(P ∥ Q) = ∫ p(x) log [p(x)/q(x)] dx

  • Where p > 0 but q → 0: the integrand p·log(p/q) → +∞. Catastrophic. So Q is forced to keep mass everywhere P has mass — zero-avoiding, mass-covering.
  • Where q > 0 but p → 0: the integrand has weight p ≈ 0, so it contributes ≈ nothing. Q putting mass in places P never visits is nearly free. That's why the lab's forward solution happily covered the empty valley.

Slogan: wherever P goes, Q must follow.

Recall 4.2Same exercise for reverse KL: integral, then the two boundary cases. Why "zero-forcing"? Why is dropping an entire mode of P completely free?

Reveal unlocks at 100 characters.

Answer

KL(Q ∥ P) = ∫ q(x) log [q(x)/p(x)] dx

  • Where q > 0 but p → 0: integrand q·log(q/p) → +∞. So Q must put zero (or near-zero) mass wherever P is tiny — it is forced toward zero outside P's high-density regions: zero-forcing.
  • Where p > 0 but q → 0: weight q ≈ 0 ⇒ contributes nothing. Q can drop entire modes of P at no cost — mode-seeking.

Slogan: Q only goes where P already is. Together: forward KL overestimates support (too broad, covers, blurs); reverse KL underestimates support (too narrow, commits, sharpens).

Part 5

Where each one lives in the wild

🔒 Locked — complete both recalls in Part 4.

The direction of the KL is never an arbitrary choice — it's usually dictated by what you can sample from, and it silently decides whether your model covers or commits. Diagnose each case before revealing.

Case 5.1Maximum likelihood estimation: you maximize the average log-likelihood Ex~data[log qθ(x)]. Which KL (between the data distribution pdata and your model qθ) is this secretly minimizing?

Correct — and verify the algebra in your head

KL(pdata ∥ q) = Epdata[log pdata] − Epdata[log q]. The first term doesn't contain θ, so minimizing the KL ⇔ maximizing Edata[log q] ⇔ MLE. (Same story for cross-entropy loss in classification.)

Consequence you can now predict: MLE-trained models are mass-covering. A language model trained purely by MLE must put probability on everything in the data distribution — including the dull, the rare, and the weird — and when its capacity falls short, it blurs across modes rather than dropping any.

Case 5.2Variational inference approximates an intractable posterior p(z|x) with a simple q(z), minimizing reverse KL(q ∥ p). Why reverse, when forward would arguably give a more honest, covering approximation?

Correct

Eq[log q/p] only needs samples from q (which you control) and p up to a normalizing constant — that's exactly the ELBO. Forward KL would need Ep[·], i.e. samples from the very posterior you can't compute. The direction is chosen by tractability, and you inherit its personality as a side effect: VI posteriors are zero-forcing, so they tend to lock onto one mode and underestimate uncertainty. Now you know why, mechanically.

Case 5.3In RLHF, the policy π is penalized with Ex~π[log π(x)/πref(x)] to keep it close to the reference model. Which direction is this, and what failure mode does its personality predict?

Correct

Sampler is π, numerator is π — reverse KL with respect to the reference. It only punishes π for doing things πref finds unlikely; it never punishes π for abandoning things πref could do. Combined with reward-maximization (which loves concentrating on high-reward outputs), this predicts the well-known diversity collapse / mode collapse of RLHF-tuned models: fewer distinct phrasings, more samey outputs. The KL direction in one penalty term shapes the personality of the whole model.

Case 5.4Your approximating family is too simple to represent all of P's modes, and a blurry average of modes would be useless — you need one sharp, coherent answer (e.g., a single plausible image, a single executable plan). Which KL do you minimize?

Correct

Reverse KL commits to one mode and stays sharp — exactly the lab's Challenge 2. This is the design rule worth keeping:

Need calibrated coverage of everything that can happen (density estimation, compression, honest uncertainty)? → forward KL.
Need one coherent, high-probability answer and can afford to drop alternatives (VI, RL-style fine-tuning, sharp generation)? → reverse KL.

Bonus thread to pull later: knowledge distillation classically uses forward KL(teacher ∥ student) so the student covers the teacher; recent LLM distillation work sometimes prefers reverse KL precisely to avoid the student blurring over teacher modes it can't fit.

Model summary — grade yourself against it

Definitions. KL(P ∥ Q) = Ex~P[log P(x)/Q(x)] ≥ 0, zero iff P = Q, asymmetric. The first argument is both the sampler and the numerator. Forward KL = KL(P ∥ Q) where P is the true/target distribution; reverse KL = KL(Q ∥ P). It measures expected excess surprise from believing Q when reality is the first argument.

Boundary mechanics. Forward: where P > 0 but Q → 0 the integrand p·log(p/q) → ∞, so Q must cover all of P's support (zero-avoiding); where P ≈ 0, anything Q does is nearly free. Reverse: where Q > 0 but P → 0 the integrand q·log(q/p) → ∞, so Q must vacate regions P doesn't occupy (zero-forcing); modes of P that Q ignores cost nothing because Q never samples them.

Bimodal target, single-Gaussian family. Forward KL → one wide Gaussian between the bumps (mass-covering / mean-seeking; for Gaussians, exactly moment matching). Reverse KL → a narrow Gaussian on one bump, either bump, other abandoned free (mode-seeking; multiple optima).

In the wild. MLE / cross-entropy = forward KL(pdata ∥ q) (the data term is the only θ-dependent part) → trained models must cover the data distribution. Variational inference and the RLHF KL penalty = reverse KL, chosen because you can only sample your own q/π — with the side effects that direction implies: variance underestimation in VI, diversity collapse in RLHF.

Mantra. Forward: wherever P goes, Q must follow. Reverse: Q only goes where P already is.
