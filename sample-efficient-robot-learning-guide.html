01How to think about the problem

“Sample efficient” is ambiguous. Pin down which budget you are minimizing, because the best technique differs for each:

BudgetWhat's scarceDominant strategy
Real robot steps / wall-clockHours on hardware, wear, resets, safety, human supervisionPush learning into simulation; offline-to-online; high update-to-data ratio
Simulator stepsGPU-hours (cheap, ~10⁶–10⁷ steps/s)Almost never the binding constraint today — parallelize
SupervisionReward functions, task specs, labelsFoundation-model-generated rewards/tasks; self-supervised goals
Human timeDemos, tuning, reward engineeringLLM reward design, automatic DR, generative simulation
The one insight that matters most

Real-world samples are ~5–7 orders of magnitude more expensive than simulated ones. A GPU simulator produces millions of steps per second; a real robot produces a few per second and needs supervision, resets, and safety. So the highest-leverage sample-efficiency decision is usually not “which RL algorithm” but “how few real samples can I get away with, and can I move the rest into sim or into priors?”

This yields five complementary pillars. Most SOTA systems combine several:

  • A. Simulation-first — learn in massively parallel sim, transfer zero-/few-shot. Default when a usable simulator exists.
  • B. Automated supervision — foundation models generate the rewards, tasks, scenes, and curricula a human would otherwise provide.
  • C. Sample-efficient RL algorithms — world models, high-UTD model-free, offline-to-online; maximum learning per transition.
  • D. Real-world autonomous RL — when you must learn on hardware, make it reset-free, safe, and bootstrap-able.
  • E. Unsupervised pretraining — exploration, intrinsic motivation, and skill discovery acquire reusable behavior with no task reward at all.

Plus a cross-cutting accelerator: representation priors (pretrained visual encoders, learned world models, offline data) that reduce the samples any downstream method needs.

A useful mental model of the cost hierarchy (cheapest → most expensive source of a learning signal):

free priors   <   sim steps   <   autonomous real steps   <   supervised real steps   <   human demos
(pretrained      (parallel      (reset-free, self-         (reward needs a human       (the thing
 encoders,        GPU sim)       scored rewards)            to score / reset)           we're avoiding)
 world models)

The whole game is to satisfy a skill requirement using sources as far to the left as possible.

02The central obstacle: the reward problem

Human demonstrations conveniently bundle three things: (1) what to do (task spec), (2) a dense learning signal (the trajectory itself), and (3) exploration guidance (states worth visiting). Remove demos and you must reconstruct all three. In practice the hardest is (2) the reward/learning signal. Everything below is, at some level, a different answer to “where does the supervision come from if not a human?”

The five demo-free sources of supervision:

  1. Engineered reward — hand-written objective. Cheap, ubiquitous in locomotion (track a velocity command, stay upright, minimize energy). Brittle for manipulation; prone to reward hacking.
  2. Foundation-model reward — an LLM writes the reward code (Eureka), or a VLM scores progress toward a goal image / gives preferences (RL-VLM-F, VLM-RM). Removes most reward engineering. → Pillar B.
  3. Self-supervised / goal-conditioned reward — the agent sets its own goals and is rewarded for reaching them (Hindsight Experience Replay, contrastive RL). Needs only a goal representation, no human labels.
  4. Success-classifier reward — learn a classifier of “goal achieved” from a handful of goal example states (not full trajectories): VICE, RCE. Far cheaper than demos.
  5. Intrinsic reward — curiosity / novelty / skill-diversity, requiring no task signal at all. → Pillar E.
Keep this taxonomy in mind

Every method in this guide slots into one of these five supervision sources plus one of the five pillars. When you read a new paper, locate it on both axes — it clarifies what the method is really buying you.

03Pillar A — Simulation-first learning (sim-to-real)default choice

When to use: a simulator (even a rough one) exists for your robot and task. This is the dominant SOTA paradigm for locomotion and increasingly for manipulation, because it reduces real sample cost to near zero — you pay only GPU time.

3.1 Massively parallel GPU simulation — the foundation

The 2022 result that reshaped the field: train a quadruped to walk in ~20 minutes on a single GPU by running thousands of environments in parallel (Rudin et al., “Learning to Walk in Minutes”). PPO is the workhorse here — not because it is sample-efficient per step (it isn't), but because in sim the binding constraint is throughput, and PPO parallelizes trivially across 4,096–65,536 envs.

Practical takeaway

The throughput problem is largely solved. With any of these you can spend billions of sim steps cheaply, so your engineering effort should go into the sim-to-real gap, not into squeezing sim-sample-efficiency.

3.2 Closing the sim-to-real gap

Four families, often stacked:

(a) Domain Randomization (DR)

Randomize physics (mass, friction, motor gains, latency, terrain) and visuals (textures, lighting) so the real world looks like just another sample from training. The original recipe (Tobin et al. 2017). Cheap and effective; the art is choosing ranges — too narrow fails to transfer, too wide makes the task unlearnable.

(b) Automatic Domain Randomization (ADR)

Start narrow and automatically widen each range as the policy masters the current distribution — an automatic curriculum over difficulty. This is how OpenAI solved Rubik's-cube manipulation on a real Shadow Hand (OpenAI 2019). Removes manual range-tuning and yields robustness fixed DR can't.

(c) Teacher–student / privileged learning

Train a teacher with privileged simulator info (true friction, contact forces, terrain heightmap, object pose) — easy and sample-cheap because the state is fully observed. Then distill into a student that only sees deployable sensors, forcing it to infer the privileged variables implicitly. This is behind most agile-locomotion results:

  • RMA — Rapid Motor Adaptation (Kumar et al. 2021): the student learns an adaptation module that estimates a latent environment-vector online from a short history, adapting to new terrain/payload in fractions of a second. The canonical demo-free legged-RL recipe.
  • ANYmal Parkour (Science Robotics 2024) and Extreme Parkour — teacher on simplified terrain, distill to a depth-image visuomotor student, optionally RL-finetune.
  • Concurrent Teacher-Student (CTS) and Regularized Online Adaptation — train teacher and student jointly to avoid the distillation gap.

(d) Real2Sim2Real / system identification

Make the simulator match your robot, then randomize around it:

  • System ID — fit physics parameters (mass, friction, motor model, latency) to real logs; tightens DR ranges around reality.
  • Differentiable simulation — backprop through physics to identify parameters from few real rollouts (very sample-efficient ID), e.g. D-REX for dexterous grasping.
  • Real-to-sim reconstruction — build interactive sim scenes from real images/video (Gaussian-splat or mesh → physics), e.g. Real2Render2Real. Generate sim training data matched to a specific real scene without a robot.
  • VLM-guided system ID — DexSim2Real uses a VLM to propose/refine sim parameters and randomization.

3.3 DrEureka — automating the entire sim-to-real loop

DrEureka (Ma et al., RSS 2024) is the current high-water mark for human-free sim-to-real. Given only the environment source and a task description, an LLM (1) writes the reward function (extending Eureka, with safety-aware instructions) and (2) uses a Reward-Aware Physics Prior (RAPP) to set sensible domain-randomization ranges automatically. It matched human-designed configs on quadruped locomotion and dexterous manipulation, and solved novel tasks (a quadruped balancing and walking on a yoga ball) with no iterative manual design and no demonstrations. The template to emulate: LLM picks the reward and the DR ranges; massively parallel sim does the rest.

  • Isaac Lab documentation & MuJoCo Playground tutorials — runnable parallel-sim training recipes.
  • DrEureka project page — code, videos, and the RAPP method.

04Pillar B — Automated supervision (replacing the human)

If demos are off the table, foundation models are the most powerful substitute for the human's role in specifying what to do and how to score it.

4.1 LLM-generated reward functions

Eureka (Ma et al., ICLR 2024) is the landmark. GPT-4, given environment source code + a task description, writes executable reward-function code, then runs an evolutionary loop: train a short RL run, feed back “reward reflection” (per-component statistics and success metrics), and mutate the reward. On 29 IsaacGym tasks it beat expert human-written rewards on 83%, +52% average normalized improvement — discovering behaviors like pen-spinning. Because reward search runs against the fast simulator, it costs LLM calls and GPU time, not real samples.

Related: Text2Reward (interpretable dense reward code), Language to Rewards (LLM emits reward parameters consumed by an MPC controller, Google DeepMind), and DrEureka (Eureka + automatic DR, §3.3).

Caveat — reward hacking

LLM rewards can be gamed by the policy (high reward, wrong behavior). The evolutionary reflection loop and explicit success-metric tracking mitigate this; always validate against a held-out success criterion you trust.

4.2 VLM / foundation-model rewards (no code, perceptual goals)

When the objective is perceptual (“the cube is in the bin”), a vision-language model can score it:

  • RL-VLM-F — a VLM gives preference feedback over image pairs (“which frame better achieves the goal?”); learn a reward model from preferences (RLHF-style, but the VLM is the labeler). No human in the loop.
  • VLM-RM — reward = CLIP similarity between the current observation and a natural-language goal; trained a MuJoCo humanoid to kneel / do the splits from a single text prompt.
  • RoboCLIP — reward from a single video or language description via video-language embedding similarity.
  • VIP / LIV — value-implicit pretraining yields a representation whose embedding distance is a goal-conditioned reward/value.

These convert “I can describe or show the goal” into a dense reward with no trajectory demos.

4.3 Generative simulation — automating tasks, scenes, and rewards

Instead of generating one reward, generate the entire training environment at scale:

  • RoboGen (Wang et al., ICML 2024): an LLM proposes tasks → generates scenes (retrieved/generated assets, plausible layouts) → decomposes into subtasks → writes rewards → learns skills. A self-driving “infinite data” pipeline with minimal human input.
  • Gen2Sim — image-to-3D asset generation + LLM task/reward generation, enabling long-horizon tasks via temporal decomposition.
  • GenSim — LLM generates manipulation task code to expand a task library 10× for multitask pretraining.

The most ambitious form of demo-free learning: the human specifies a domain, and the system manufactures tasks, supervision, and data indefinitely. Trade-off: generated tasks vary in quality/realism — pair with sim-to-real (Pillar A) for deployment.

4.4 Zero-shot composition (skills with no training at all)

For some manipulation tasks you can skip policy learning entirely: Code as Policies (LLM writes the control program from language + perception APIs) and VoxPoser (LLM + VLM compose 3D affordance/constraint value-maps an optimizer turns into trajectories; zero-shot, training-free). The most sample-efficient possible answer (zero samples) when the task decomposes into known primitives — and a strong way to bootstrap data for the learning methods elsewhere in this guide.

Further reading — foundation models for supervision
  • Eureka project page and Language-to-Rewards project page — code + qualitative reward-generation demos.
  • “A Step Toward World Models: A Survey on Robotic Manipulation” (2025) — situates generative-simulation and foundation-model methods in context.

05Pillar C — Sample-efficient RL algorithms

This pillar extracts maximum learning per transition — decisive when transitions are real-robot samples, and helpful even in sim. Two big levers: model-based imagination and high update-to-data (UTD) off-policy learning.

5.1 Model-based RL / world models usually the most sample-efficient

A learned world model lets the agent generate vast amounts of imagined experience from few real transitions — the core reason model-based methods dominate sample-efficiency benchmarks.

  • DreamerV3 (Hafner et al.; Nature 2025): learns a latent Recurrent State-Space Model from pixels, trains an actor-critic purely in imagined latent rollouts. Robustness tricks (symlog, KL balancing, return normalization) let one fixed hyperparameter set work across 150+ tasks — it even mined a Minecraft diamond from scratch. Excellent default for pixel-based, partially observed problems.
  • TD-MPC2 (Hansen et al., ICLR 2024): combines a learned latent implicit world model with short-horizon MPC planning plus TD-learned value and policy priors. Building on TD-MPC, on 104 continuous-control tasks it beats SAC and DreamerV3 in both data-efficiency and final performance, one config; a single 317M-param agent does 80 tasks. Current SOTA for state-based continuous control sample-efficiency.
  • DINO-WM (2024): builds the world model on frozen pretrained DINOv2 features → zero-shot planning for new goals. A bridge between Pillars C and §8.
  • MBPO, PETS, and Plan2Explore — Dyna-style and ensemble/planning predecessors; Plan2Explore also gives task-free exploration (§7).
Rule of thumb

For the fewest real samples, start with a world-model method: TD-MPC2 for state-based control, DreamerV3 for pixels.

5.2 High-UTD model-free off-policy RL

You can approach model-based efficiency with model-free methods by doing many gradient updates per environment step (high UTD ratio) — but naive high UTD causes value overestimation and collapse. The fix is regularization/ensembling:

MethodKey ideaWhy it helps sample efficiency
SACOff-policy, entropy-regularized actor-criticThe robust workhorse baseline
TD3Twin critics, delayed policy updatesTames overestimation
REDQEnsemble of ~10 Q-nets, random subset for target, UTD≈20Matches model-based efficiency, model-free
DroQDropout + LayerNorm critics, small ensemble, high UTDREDQ-level efficiency at far lower compute
CrossQBatchNorm in critics, no target networks, UTD≈1REDQ-level efficiency, lower compute, simpler
TQCTruncated quantile (distributional) criticsFine-grained overestimation control
Practical guidance

On real hardware, DroQ or CrossQ with UTD 10–20 is a strong, compute-reasonable default. LayerNorm in the critic is one of the highest-value single tricks for stabilizing high-UTD off-policy learning.

5.3 Offline-to-online: bootstrap with autonomously collected data

If you have any prior dataset — crucially, this can be autonomously collected (scripted, sim-generated, random, prior policies), not human demos — you can pretrain offline then finetune online with very few real samples:

  • RLPD (Ball et al. 2023): no offline pretraining phase — just symmetrically sample 50/50 from the offline and online buffers during off-policy learning, with LayerNorm + high UTD + ensembles. Extremely sample-efficient and robust to suboptimal offline data. The backbone of several real-world systems below.
  • Cal-QL — calibrated offline pretraining that finetunes online without the usual unlearning dip; also IQL / AWAC for warm-starting.
  • SimLauncher (2025): use a sim-pretrained policy to both explore and generate rollouts that bootstrap the critic for real-world RL — sim providing the “prior data” with no human demos.
Key reframe

“Offline RL needs a dataset” does not mean “needs human demos.” Sim rollouts, scripted controllers, prior policies, and autonomous play all qualify — and offline-to-online is among the most reliable ways to cut real-world sample cost.

5.4 Pixel-based efficiency: augmentation & auxiliary objectives

Learning policies directly from images is sample-hungry unless you add: DrQ-v2 / DrQ (random-shift image augmentation on critic targets — the biggest, cheapest win for pixel RL), RAD (broader augmentation), and CURL (contrastive auxiliary loss). Best modern practice: augmentation + a pretrained visual encoder (§8) rather than learning vision from scratch.

06Pillar D — Real-world autonomous RL

Sometimes the sim gap is too large (deformables, fluids, fine contact) and you must learn on hardware. The goal: make real-world RL autonomous (no babysitting), safe, and bootstrapped so the real-sample budget is hours, not weeks.

6.1 Reset-free / autonomous learning

The hidden cost of real-world RL is resets — a human re-arranging the scene between episodes. Reset-free RL learns a reset (backward) policy alongside the forward (task) policy, so the robot returns itself to a start state:

  • Leave No Trace (Eysenbach et al.) — joint forward + reset policies; the reset policy also acts as a safety/reversibility signal.
  • VaPRL (value-accelerated persistent RL via subgoal curricula) and MEDAL (state-distribution matching for non-episodic RL) — perpetual training with ~1000× less human intervention; benchmarked in EARL (Environments for Autonomous RL).
  • Reset-free RL with world models (2025) — combine §5.1 imagination with autonomy.

6.2 SERL — a practical real-world RL stack

SERL (Luo et al. 2024) packages RLPD + reward classifiers + careful engineering into a software suite that learns real-world manipulation (PCB insertion, cable routing, assembly) in ~1–2 hours of real time.

Demo-free configuration note

Headline SERL / HIL-SERL results often use a handful of demos or human interventions (HIL = human-in-the-loop). To stay demo-free, replace those with: a learned success classifier from goal images (VICE/RCE-style, §2), scripted/sim-bootstrapped prior data for RLPD, and reset-free automation. The algorithmic core (RLPD + classifier reward) is fully compatible with zero demos.

6.3 RL on top of generalist policies (latent-space RL)

A powerful 2025 pattern: don't learn from scratch — take a base policy and steer it with a small amount of real RL:

  • DSRL — Diffusion Steering via RL (Wagenmaker et al., CoRL 2025): freeze a base diffusion/flow policy and run RL only over its latent-noise input — training a tiny MLP to pick the noise that yields better actions. Needs only black-box access (no weights, no backprop through the base), is highly sample-efficient, and enables real-world autonomous improvement. Demo-free caveat: the base diffusion policy is usually BC-trained on demos; to stay demo-free, source the base from a sim-trained or generative-simulation policy (Pillars A/B), then let DSRL adapt it to reality.
  • RL-100 (2025) follows the same “RL-refines-a-base-policy” philosophy for high-reliability manipulation.

This “policy prior + thin RL adaptation layer” is, in 2025–2026, often the most real-sample-efficient route for contact-rich manipulation.

  • SERL and HIL-SERL project pages — open-source code, hardware setup, and reward-classifier tooling.
  • EARL benchmark — formalism + environments for autonomous (reset-minimizing) RL.
  • DSRL project page — latent-space RL fine-tuning of diffusion/flow policies.

07Pillar E — Unsupervised pretraining, exploration & skill discovery

These methods acquire useful behavior with no task reward — the purest form of demo-free learning. Use them to pretrain a policy/representation that a downstream task then finetunes with very few samples.

7.1 Intrinsic motivation / exploration

When rewards are sparse or absent, an intrinsic reward drives the agent to gather informative experience:

  • ICM (curiosity) — reward = prediction error of a learned forward dynamics model in a feature space.
  • RND — reward = error predicting a fixed random network's output (novelty bonus). Simple, robust, widely used.
  • Plan2Explore — model-based, task-agnostic: explore to maximize model disagreement, building a world model that enables zero-shot adaptation to new rewards. Bridges Pillars C and E.

7.2 Unsupervised skill discovery

Learn a repertoire of distinguishable skills (a skill-conditioned policy π(a|s,z)) by maximizing the mutual information between skills z and the states they visit — no reward needed. Downstream tasks then plan/RL over the small skill space → a big sample-efficiency win.

  • DIAYN (“Diversity is All You Need”) — the seminal MI-maximization method.
  • DADS — dynamics-aware skills, directly usable for model-based planning.
  • CIC — contrastive intrinsic control; scales skill count.
  • METRA (Park et al., ICLR 2024) — metric-aware discovery using a temporal-distance (Lipschitz) objective; the most scalable approach, covering state space far better than MI methods. The current go-to for unsupervised skill pretraining. Builds on LSD.
  • DUSDi (Disentangled USD, NeurIPS 2024) — disentangled skills for efficient hierarchical RL.
  • Emergent Real-World Skills via Unsupervised Off-Policy RL — DIAYN-style discovery run directly on a real robot.

7.3 Goal-conditioned & self-supervised RL

Turn reaching self-proposed goals into the learning signal: HER — Hindsight Experience Replay relabels failed trajectories with the goal that was achieved, so every rollout yields positive signal — making sparse-reward goal-reaching sample-efficient with no reward shaping. Contrastive RL and automatic-goal-generation (asymmetric self-play) extend this with self-curricula.

7.4 The unsupervised → task pipeline

The intended usage: (1) unsupervised exploration/skill-discovery pretraining (free, no reward) → (2) few-sample finetuning on the actual task. The URLB (Unsupervised RL Benchmark) formalizes this and shows large downstream sample savings. Pair §7.2 skills with §5.1 world models for the strongest task-time efficiency.

Further reading — exploration & skill discovery
  • Lilian Weng — “Exploration Strategies in Deep RL” (curiosity, novelty, information-gain methods, well organized).
  • URLB — benchmark + open-source baselines for eight unsupervised RL methods.

08Representation priors that cut sample cost

Orthogonal to which RL method you use: starting from pretrained representations reduces the samples any method needs, because the agent no longer learns vision/dynamics from scratch.

Pretrained visual encoders (freeze and build on top)

  • R3M — time-contrastive + video-language pretraining on Ego4D human video; +20% task success vs. from-scratch, and a strong frozen encoder for RL.
  • VC-1 — masked-autoencoder on 4,000+ hrs egocentric video + ImageNet; general embodied-AI backbone (CortexBench).
  • MVP — masked visual pretraining for motor control.
  • Theia — distills multiple vision foundation models into one rich encoder; strong robot-learning performance at smaller size / less data.
  • DINOv2 — general self-supervised features that underpin DINO-WM and many recent policies.

Learned world models & offline data as priors

DINO-WM (§5.1) builds the dynamics model on frozen DINOv2 features, enabling zero-shot planning. Generally, don't relearn perception inside the RL loop if a pretrained encoder will do. And any autonomously gathered dataset warm-starts offline-to-online RL (§5.3).

Practical default

Freeze a pretrained encoder (Theia / VC-1 / DINOv2), run RL on top with image augmentation (§5.4). This combination alone often halves sample requirements for pixel-based tasks.

09Putting it together: recipes & decision tree

9.1 Decision tree

Does a usable simulator exist for the task?
├── YES → SIM-FIRST (Pillar A). Default; real-sample cost ≈ 0.
│         • Reward known/engineerable? → write it, or use Eureka / DrEureka (B).
│         • Massively parallel PPO in Isaac Lab / MuJoCo Playground.
│         • Locomotion / partial observability → teacher–student + RMA-style adaptation.
│         • Deploy with DR / ADR; tighten with system-ID / real2sim if the gap is large.
│         • Residual reality gap? → thin real-world RL adaptation (DSRL / RLPD finetune) (C/D).
│
└── NO (or sim gap is fundamental: deformables, fluids, fine contact)
          → REAL-WORLD AUTONOMOUS RL (Pillars C + D)
          • Get a base policy cheaply: scripted, VoxPoser / Code-as-Policies, or rough sim.
          • Bootstrap with autonomous/sim data via RLPD (offline-to-online, no demos).
          • Reward without demos: VLM/CLIP reward (B) or a success classifier from goal images.
          • Make it reset-free; high-UTD off-policy (DroQ/CrossQ) or a world model (TD-MPC2/Dreamer).
          • Steer a base diffusion/flow policy with DSRL for the fewest real samples.

Across both branches, ALWAYS stack the free accelerators:
  • Pretrained visual encoder (Theia / VC-1 / DINOv2), frozen, + image augmentation.
  • Unsupervised skill/exploration pretraining (METRA / Plan2Explore) when reward is sparse.
  • A learned world model to multiply each real transition into many imagined ones.

9.2 Worked recipe — legged locomotion zero demos, ~zero real samples

  1. Sim: Isaac Lab or MuJoCo Playground, thousands of parallel envs.
  2. Reward: engineer (velocity tracking + upright + energy/smoothness), or let DrEureka write it.
  3. Privilege: train a teacher with privileged terrain/dynamics state (PPO).
  4. Distill: student on proprioception (+ depth for parkour) via RMA-style online adaptation.
  5. Robustness: ADR over mass, friction, motor gains, latency, terrain.
  6. Deploy: zero-shot. Optional thin real-world finetune for residual gap.

Outcome class: the standard pipeline behind ANYmal / Go2 / G1 / H1 walking and parkour — no demonstrations, deployment-ready from sim.

9.3 Worked recipe — contact-rich manipulation (insertion, assembly)

  • If sim is faithful enough: sim-first with an Eureka reward + ADR, then DSRL / RLPD adaptation on hardware for the contact residual.
  • If the sim gap is fundamental: real-world RLPD (offline-to-online) seeded with scripted/sim “prior data”; reward = VLM preference (RL-VLM-F) or a success classifier from a few goal images (no demos); reset-free; DroQ/CrossQ critic with LayerNorm + augmentation + frozen pretrained encoder. Budget: hours of real time.

9.4 Worked recipe — dexterous in-hand manipulation

OpenAI-style: massively parallel sim + ADR + teacher–student with online adaptation, reward via Eureka. This produced real-hand Rubik's-cube and pen-spinning skills with no human demonstrations. Add real2sim system-ID if your hand's dynamics are idiosyncratic.

10Pitfalls and practical engineering

  • Reward hacking — the dominant failure of demo-free RL. The policy maximizes the letter, not the intent, of an engineered/LLM reward. Mitigate: hold out an independent success metric; use Eureka's reflection loop; prefer success classifiers + sparse terminal reward over heavily shaped dense reward where possible.
  • Sim-to-real gap sources — actuator dynamics & latency, contact/friction modeling, sensor noise, unmodeled compliance. Latency and motor models dominate in locomotion; contact in manipulation. Randomize these aggressively (and identify them via system-ID).
  • High-UTD instability — without LayerNorm/ensembles/regularization, many updates per step diverge via Q-overestimation. Use REDQ/DroQ/CrossQ recipes; don't crank UTD on a vanilla SAC.
  • DR-range Goldilocks problem — too narrow → no transfer; too wide → unlearnable. Prefer ADR (auto-curriculum) or RAPP (DrEureka) over hand-picked ranges.
  • “Demo-free” leaks — audit for hidden human supervision: BC base policies (diffusion policies), demo-seeded buffers, human-labeled rewards, manual resets. Each has a demo-free substitute above (sim base policy, autonomous/sim prior data, VLM/classifier reward, reset-free).
  • Generative-sim quality variance — LLM-proposed tasks/scenes/rewards range from excellent to nonsensical; filter with execution feedback and success checks before trusting them.
  • Evaluation honesty — report real-world samples and wall-clock separately from sim steps; success rates on held-out object/terrain configs, not the training distribution.
  • Safety on hardware — torque/velocity limits, reversibility (reset-policy as safety signal), and a human e-stop are prerequisites for autonomous real-world RL.

Executive summary (one paragraph)

To make robot skill learning maximally sample-efficient without human demonstrations: default to simulation — train in a massively parallel GPU simulator where real-sample cost is ~zero, let an LLM write the reward and domain-randomization ranges (Eureka/DrEureka), and transfer with domain randomization + teacher–student/RMA adaptation. When the sim gap is fundamental and you must touch hardware, minimize real samples with a world-model or high-UTD off-policy learner (TD-MPC2/DreamerV3 or DroQ/CrossQ), bootstrap from autonomously collected prior data (RLPD, not demos), make learning reset-free, source rewards from a VLM or a success classifier rather than demos, and prefer steering a sim/generated base policy with thin latent-space RL (DSRL). Throughout, stack the free accelerators — frozen pretrained visual encoders, image augmentation, and unsupervised exploration/skill-discovery pretraining (METRA/Plan2Explore).
