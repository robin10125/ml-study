Chapter 4 · The Step-Size Problem & Surrogate Objectives

This is the conceptual heart of the course. Three results — the performance difference lemma, the surrogate objective, and their first-order agreement — explain precisely why naive policy gradients are fragile, and define the quantity that both TRPO and PPO actually optimize. Master this chapter and those two algorithms become near-corollaries.

4.1 Why one bad step can end a run

In supervised learning, an oversized step costs you one bad update; the next minibatch comes from the same distribution and pulls you back. In RL the data comes from the policy (§1.4). The failure mode is a ratchet:

The policy-collapse ratchet

Oversized step → policy becomes much worse → the worse policy visits bad, uninformative states → gradients estimated from those states say nothing about how to return to good behavior → further updates wander. The step destroyed not just the parameters but the future data. Recovery is slow or never.

Worse, the right step size is not knowable in parameter space. The map from parameters to behavior is wildly non-uniform: for a Gaussian policy, shifting \(\mu\) by 0.1 is a negligible behavioral change when \(\sigma = 10\) and a revolution when \(\sigma = 0.01\) — identical parameter distances, orders-of-magnitude different distribution distances. A fixed learning rate that is safe in one region of \(\theta\)-space is reckless in another. Conclusion: step sizes must be measured in distribution space (KL divergence), not parameter space. That idea, made rigorous, is TRPO; made cheap, it is PPO.

Explain (a) why an oversized step is far more damaging in on-policy RL than in supervised learning, and (b) why no fixed parameter-space learning rate can be right, with the Gaussian-policy example and the conclusion it forces.

(a) What generates the next batch of training data in each setting? (b) Compare \(\Delta\mu = 0.1\) at \(\sigma=10\) vs \(\sigma=0.01\).

(a) Supervised data is i.i.d. from a fixed distribution, so a bad step is corrected by subsequent unaffected batches. In RL the post-step policy generates the next data; a collapsed policy collects states that carry no signal about recovering, so the damage compounds. (b) The same parameter displacement can be behaviorally tiny (\(\sigma=10\): distributions overlap almost completely) or enormous (\(\sigma=0.01\): essentially disjoint). Since the parameter→behavior map's sensitivity varies across \(\theta\)-space, safety must be defined by a distribution distance (KL) — a trust region in policy space, not parameter space.

4.2 The performance difference lemma

How much better is a new policy \(\pi'\) than the current \(\pi\)? There is an exact, beautiful answer (Kakade & Langford, 2002). Define the discounted state-visitation measure \(d^{\pi'}\!(s) = \sum_{t}\gamma^t \Pr(s_t = s \mid \pi')\) and write \(\eta(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi}[\sum_t \gamma^t r_t]\) for expected return.

Performance difference lemma
$$ \eta(\pi') - \eta(\pi) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi'}\Big[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t A^{\pi}(s_t, a_t) \Big] \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\pi'}}\,\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi'(\cdot|s)}\big[ A^\pi(s,a) \big]. $$

In words: the improvement of \(\pi'\) over \(\pi\) equals the advantage of the old policy, accumulated along the new policy's trajectories. Run \(\pi'\); every time it picks an action that \(\pi\) considers above-average, that's pure profit; below-average, pure loss; total profit = total improvement, exactly.

Proof. The key trick: write \(A^\pi(s_t,a_t) = \mathbb{E}_{s_{t+1}}[r_t + \gamma V^\pi(s_{t+1}) - V^\pi(s_t)]\) (the identity from §1.5) and evaluate the sum along a trajectory of \(\pi'\):

$$ \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi'}\Big[\sum_t \gamma^t A^\pi(s_t,a_t)\Big] = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi'}\Big[\sum_t \gamma^t\big( r_t + \gamma V^\pi(s_{t+1}) - V^\pi(s_t) \big)\Big]. $$

The value terms telescope: \(\sum_t \gamma^t(\gamma V^\pi(s_{t+1}) - V^\pi(s_t)) = -V^\pi(s_0)\) (each \(+\gamma^{t+1}V^\pi(s_{t+1})\) cancels the next term's \(-\gamma^{t+1}V^\pi(s_{t+1})\); the tail vanishes by discounting). So the right side equals

$$ \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi'}\Big[\sum_t \gamma^t r_t\Big] - \mathbb{E}_{s_0\sim\rho_0}\big[V^\pi(s_0)\big] = \eta(\pi') - \eta(\pi), $$

using the fact that both policies share the same initial-state distribution. \(\blacksquare\)

Note the structural rhyme with §3.4: GAE's telescoping collapsed sums of TD errors; here the same cancellation collapses a sum of advantages. One algebraic move, two pillars of the theory.

Replace \(A^\pi\) by its TD form \(r + \gamma V^\pi(s') - V^\pi(s)\), valid in expectation, then telescope the value terms along the trajectory.
\(\sum_t \gamma^t(\gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t))\): write out \(t=0,1,2\) and watch the cancellation; only \(-V(s_0)\) survives.

\(\eta(\pi') - \eta(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi'}[\sum_t \gamma^t A^\pi(s_t,a_t)]\): old policy's advantage on the new policy's trajectories. Proof: \(A^\pi(s_t,a_t) = \mathbb{E}[r_t + \gamma V^\pi(s_{t+1}) - V^\pi(s_t)]\); summing with weights \(\gamma^t\) along \(\tau\sim\pi'\), the value terms telescope to \(-V^\pi(s_0)\), leaving \(\mathbb{E}_{\pi'}[\sum\gamma^t r_t] - \mathbb{E}_{s_0}[V^\pi(s_0)]\). The first term is \(\eta(\pi')\); the second is \(\eta(\pi)\) because \(s_0 \sim \rho_0\) for both policies — the initial distribution belongs to the environment. ∎

4.3 The surrogate: optimize the new policy on the old policy's data

The lemma suggests an algorithm: pick \(\pi'\) to maximize \(\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi'}}\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi'}[A^\pi(s,a)]\). One obstacle makes this non-trivial — and it is the obstacle of policy optimization: the state distribution \(d^{\pi'}\) belongs to the policy we haven't chosen yet. We cannot sample from it without running \(\pi'\), and we can't run every candidate \(\pi'\).

The pragmatic move: pretend the state distribution doesn't change. Replace \(d^{\pi'}\) by \(d^{\pi}\) — but correct the action distribution exactly, using importance sampling:

$$ \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi'(\cdot|s)}\big[A^\pi(s,a)\big] = \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi(\cdot|s)}\Big[ \frac{\pi'(a|s)}{\pi(a|s)}\, A^\pi(s,a) \Big], $$

valid whenever \(\pi(a|s) \gt 0\) wherever \(\pi'(a|s) \gt 0\) (multiply and divide inside the expectation — that's the whole proof). This yields the object TRPO and PPO both optimize:

The surrogate objective
$$ L_\pi(\pi') \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^\pi}\, \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi(\cdot|s)}\Big[ \frac{\pi'(a|s)}{\pi(a|s)}\, A^\pi(s,a) \Big]. $$

Every ingredient is estimable from data collected under the current policy \(\pi\): states from \(d^\pi\), actions from \(\pi\), advantages \(\hat A_t\) from GAE, and the ratio is a forward pass through both policy heads. With samples: \(\hat L(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_t \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t|s_t)}\hat A_t = \frac{1}{N}\sum_t r_t(\theta)\hat A_t\).

The substitution \(d^{\pi'} \to d^\pi\) — only the state distribution is approximated; actions are handled exactly — is where all the error lives. Everything in TRPO and PPO is about controlling that error.

Construct the surrogate from memory: start from the performance difference lemma, identify which factor is intractable and why, make the approximation, and apply importance sampling to the part that can be handled exactly. State the support condition IS requires, and what the ratio \(r_t(\theta)\) is concretely in code.

Two distributions in the lemma belong to \(\pi'\): states and actions. One of them can be exactly corrected from \(\pi\)'s samples, one cannot. Which, and why?

Lemma: \(\Delta\eta = \mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi'}}\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi'}[A^\pi]\). Sampling \(d^{\pi'}\) requires deploying \(\pi'\) — intractable during optimization over candidate \(\pi'\). Approximate \(d^{\pi'}\!\approx d^\pi\); the action expectation is corrected exactly: \(\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi'}[A^\pi] = \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[\tfrac{\pi'}{\pi}A^\pi]\), requiring \(\pi \gt 0\) wherever \(\pi' \gt 0\). Result: \(L_\pi(\pi') = \mathbb{E}_{d^\pi,\pi}[\tfrac{\pi'(a|s)}{\pi(a|s)}A^\pi]\). In code, \(r_t(\theta) = \exp(\log\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) - \log\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(a_t|s_t))\) — new log-prob minus stored rollout log-prob, exponentiated.

4.4 The license to use \(L\): first-order agreement

Why is maximizing \(L\) a sensible proxy for maximizing \(\eta\)? Because at the current policy the two functions touch tangentially. Parametrize \(\pi_\theta\) and write \(L_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\theta) := L_{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}(\pi_\theta)\):

Value and gradient match at \(\theta_{\text{old}}\)
$$ L_{\theta_\text{old}}(\theta_\text{old}) = 0 = \eta(\theta_\text{old}) - \eta(\theta_\text{old}), \qquad \nabla_\theta L_{\theta_\text{old}}(\theta)\big|_{\theta_\text{old}} = \nabla_\theta\, \eta(\theta)\big|_{\theta_\text{old}}. $$

Value: at \(\theta = \theta_\text{old}\) the ratio is 1, and \(\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[A^\pi] = 0\) (§1.5). Gradient: \(\nabla_\theta \frac{\pi_\theta(a|s)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(a|s)}\big|_{\theta_\text{old}} = \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a|s)|_{\theta_\text{old}}}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(a|s)} = \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a|s)\big|_{\theta_\text{old}}\), so \(\nabla L|_{\theta_\text{old}} = \mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi_\theta\, A^\pi]\) — precisely the advantage-form policy gradient of §3.2.

So: an infinitesimal ascent step on \(L\) is identical to a policy-gradient step on \(\eta\). The surrogate is not an alternative objective; it is a local model of the true one — accurate to first order at the expansion point, increasingly wrong as \(\pi_\theta\) departs from \(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\) and the frozen state distribution \(d^{\pi_\text{old}}\) stops describing where \(\pi_\theta\) actually goes. Like any local model, it has a trust radius. How far can you push before the model lies? Measured in what units? That is Chapter 5's question, and "KL divergence" is its answer.

Prove both matching properties at \(\theta_\text{old}\): (a) \(L_{\theta_\text{old}}(\theta_\text{old}) = 0\); (b) \(\nabla_\theta L|_{\theta_\text{old}} = \nabla_\theta\eta|_{\theta_\text{old}}\). Then state, in one careful sentence, the precise sense in which the surrogate can be trusted and the sense in which it cannot.

(a) needs \(\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[A^\pi(s,a)]=0\) from §1.5. (b) needs \(\nabla\pi/\pi = \nabla\log\pi\) and recognizing the §3.2 gradient.

(a) At \(\theta_\text{old}\), ratio \(\equiv 1\), so \(L = \mathbb{E}_{s}\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[A^\pi] = \mathbb{E}_s[0] = 0\). (b) Only the ratio depends on \(\theta\): its gradient at \(\theta_\text{old}\) is \(\nabla\pi_\theta/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}} = \nabla\log\pi_\theta\), giving \(\nabla L = \mathbb{E}_{d^\pi,\pi}[\nabla\log\pi_\theta\, A^\pi]\), the advantage-form policy gradient of \(\eta\). Trust: \(L\) predicts the effect of infinitesimal policy changes exactly (same gradient); it cannot be trusted for finite steps, because it evaluates the new policy under the old policy's state distribution, an error growing with the distance between policies.

4.5 Measuring policy change: KL divergence

The natural distance-like measure between the old and new action distributions at a state:

$$ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot|s)\,\big\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot|s)\big) = \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_\text{old}(\cdot|s)}\Big[ \log\frac{\pi_\text{old}(a|s)}{\pi_\theta(a|s)} \Big] \ge 0, $$

zero iff the distributions coincide (almost everywhere). Properties that matter here: it is asymmetric (TRPO uses old-first); it is parametrization-invariant — it sees only the distributions, so it is exactly the behavioral measure §4.1 demanded, immune to the \(\sigma=10\) vs \(\sigma=0.01\) trap; and for small parameter displacements it is locally quadratic, \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}} \| \pi_{\theta_\text{old}+\Delta}) \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta^\top F\, \Delta\), where \(F = \mathbb{E}\big[\nabla\log\pi\,\nabla\log\pi^\top\big]\) is the Fisher information matrix. (First-order term vanishes — KL has a minimum, value 0, at \(\Delta = 0\).) This quadratic form is the bridge to TRPO's natural gradient.

(b) Why is parametrization invariance the property that makes KL the right trust-region currency (connect to §4.1)? (c) What is the second-order expansion of KL in \(\Delta\theta\), why is there no first-order term, and what is the matrix called?

(c) \(g(\Delta) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\|\pi_{\theta_\text{old}+\Delta})\) is minimized at \(\Delta = 0\) with value 0. What does that say about \(\nabla g(0)\)?

(a) \(\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_\text{old}}[\log(\pi_\text{old}(a|s)/\pi_\theta(a|s))]\). (b) It depends only on the distributions, not on \(\theta\)'s coordinates — the same behavioral change yields the same KL whether the network is over- or under-sensitive there, fixing exactly the non-uniformity that makes parameter-space step sizes meaningless. (c) \(D_{\mathrm{KL}} \approx \frac{1}{2}\Delta^\top F \Delta\) with \(F = \mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi\,\nabla\log\pi^\top]\), the Fisher information matrix; the linear term vanishes because KL attains its minimum (0) at \(\Delta=0\), so its gradient there is zero.
