Chapter 2 · The Policy Gradient Theorem

We want \(\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\), but \(J\) is an expectation over trajectories that pass through a black-box, non-differentiable environment. One identity — the log-derivative trick — turns this impossible gradient into something you can estimate from rollouts. PPO's loss function is this identity wearing three layers of refinement.

2.1 Why you can't just backprop

\(J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\theta}[R(\tau)]\). To differentiate an expectation whose distribution depends on \(\theta\), the supervised-learning reflex — push the gradient inside onto the integrand — fails: \(R(\tau)\) doesn't depend on \(\theta\) at all (it's a sum of environment rewards), the probability of sampling \(\tau\) does. And that probability contains \(P(s'|s,a)\), which we cannot differentiate because we don't know it: the environment is a simulator or the real world, not part of the computation graph.

So we need a gradient estimator that (a) never differentiates the environment, and (b) can be computed from sampled trajectories. The score function estimator delivers both.

2.2 The log-derivative trick

The log-derivative (score function) trick

For any distribution \(p_\theta(x)\) and any function \(f(x)\) not depending on \(\theta\):

$$ \nabla_\theta\, \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}[f(x)] \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}\big[ f(x)\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x) \big]. $$

Proof — three steps, each one line:

$$ \nabla_\theta \int p_\theta(x) f(x)\, dx = \int \nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)\, f(x)\, dx = \int p_\theta(x)\, \frac{\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)}{p_\theta(x)}\, f(x)\, dx = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}\!\big[ f(x) \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x) \big], $$

using \(\nabla \log p = \nabla p / p\) in the last step. The result is again an expectation under \(p_\theta\) — so it can be estimated by sampling.

Intuition: to increase \(\mathbb{E}[f]\), raise the log-probability of samples in proportion to how good they were. Good samples get pulled toward you, bad ones (if \(f\) can be negative) get pushed away. No gradient of \(f\) — and hence no gradient of the environment — is ever needed.

State and prove the log-derivative trick from memory. Then answer: why is it essential that the final expression is an expectation under \(p_\theta\), and which step of the proof would break if \(f\) itself depended on \(\theta\)?

Write \(\nabla_\theta p_\theta = p_\theta \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta\) and substitute.

\(\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}[f] = \int \nabla_\theta p_\theta f = \int p_\theta (\nabla_\theta p_\theta / p_\theta) f = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta]\). Being an expectation under \(p_\theta\) means Monte-Carlo samples from the current policy estimate it unbiasedly — that is what makes it implementable. If \(f\) depended on \(\theta\), the very first step (product rule) would add a second term \(\mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta f]\); in RL, \(R(\tau)\) is a function of the sampled trajectory only, so this term is absent.

2.3 The policy gradient over trajectories

Apply the trick with \(x = \tau\), \(f = R\). We need \(\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)\). Take the log of the trajectory factorization from §1.3:

$$ \log p_\theta(\tau) = \log \rho_0(s_0) + \sum_{t} \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) + \sum_t \log P(s_{t+1}|s_t,a_t). $$

Differentiate: \(\rho_0\) and \(P\) don't contain \(\theta\), so the environment vanishes from the gradient:

$$ \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau) = \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t | s_t). $$
Policy gradient, trajectory form
$$ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\Big[ R(\tau) \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) \Big]. $$

This is remarkable: the gradient of expected return through an unknown, stochastic, non-differentiable world equals a weighted sum of policy log-prob gradients — all quantities we can compute. The price is paid in variance, which the rest of the course works down.

Derive the trajectory-form policy gradient from scratch: start from \(J(\theta)\), apply the log-derivative trick, expand \(\log p_\theta(\tau)\), and show exactly where and why the environment dynamics \(P\) drop out.

The log of a product is a sum; differentiate term by term and ask which terms contain \(\theta\).

\(\nabla J = \mathbb{E}[R(\tau)\nabla\log p_\theta(\tau)]\) by the trick. \(\log p_\theta(\tau) = \log\rho_0 + \sum_t \log\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) + \sum_t \log P(s_{t+1}|s_t,a_t)\). Under \(\nabla_\theta\), the \(\rho_0\) and \(P\) terms are constants (the environment doesn't depend on the policy parameters), leaving \(\sum_t \nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)\). Hence \(\nabla J = \mathbb{E}_\tau[R(\tau)\sum_t \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)]\). The dynamics drop out at the differentiation step — not by assumption but by the structure of the factorization.

2.4 A lemma you will use constantly: \(\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi] = 0\)

EGLP lemma (expected grad-log-prob)

For any distribution \(p_\theta\): \(\;\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\theta}\big[ \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x) \big] = 0.\)

Proof: \(\int p_\theta(x) dx = 1\). Differentiate both sides: \(\int \nabla_\theta p_\theta = 0\); rewrite the integrand as \(p_\theta \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta\); read the left side as an expectation. \(\blacksquare\)

Consequence: you may add or subtract any action-independent quantity inside the policy gradient without changing its expectation — because that quantity multiplies a term whose expectation is zero. This single lemma legitimizes reward-to-go (below), baselines (§3.1), and advantage normalization's mean-shift (§7.2). It is verified to machine precision by test_ppo_core.py (PASS 6).

State the EGLP lemma, prove it in two lines, and name the three places in this course where it justifies modifying the policy gradient.

Differentiate the normalization constraint \(\int p_\theta = 1\).

\(\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[\nabla\log p_\theta]=0\): differentiating \(\int p_\theta = 1\) gives \(\int \nabla p_\theta = \int p_\theta \nabla\log p_\theta = \mathbb{E}[\nabla\log p_\theta] = 0\). It licenses (1) dropping past rewards (reward-to-go), (2) subtracting a state-dependent baseline \(b(s)\), (3) shifting advantages by their batch mean when normalizing — all without biasing the gradient.

2.5 Causality: reward-to-go

In the trajectory form, the action at time \(t\) is multiplied by the total return \(R(\tau)\) — including rewards earned before \(t\). But an action cannot influence the past. The math agrees: those terms have zero expectation (each past reward is a constant with respect to the action's distribution, so EGLP kills it), and removing them strictly reduces variance:

Policy gradient, reward-to-go form
$$ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_\theta}\Big[ \sum_{t} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)\; \hat{R}_t \Big], \qquad \hat R_t = \sum_{k \ge t} \gamma^{k-t} r_k. $$

Sketch of why dropping the past is valid: fix \(t\) and a past reward \(r_j\), \(j \lt t\). Condition on everything up to \(s_t\); then \(r_j\) is a known constant \(c\), and \(\mathbb{E}_{a_t\sim\pi(\cdot|s_t)}[c\,\nabla\log\pi(a_t|s_t)] = c \cdot 0\) by EGLP. Summing over all past terms removes them from the expectation. What remains — the future — is genuinely correlated with \(a_t\) and must stay.

Prove that replacing \(R(\tau)\) with \(\hat R_t\) in the policy gradient leaves it unbiased. Where exactly does the conditioning argument use the EGLP lemma, and why can't the same argument remove future rewards?

Condition on the trajectory prefix up to and including \(s_t\). What is \(r_j\) (for \(j \lt t\)) under that conditioning?

For \(j \lt t\): \(\mathbb{E}[r_j \nabla\log\pi(a_t|s_t)] = \mathbb{E}_{\text{prefix}}\big[ r_j\, \mathbb{E}_{a_t\sim\pi(\cdot|s_t)}[\nabla\log\pi(a_t|s_t)] \big] = \mathbb{E}_{\text{prefix}}[r_j \cdot 0] = 0\): conditioned on the prefix, \(r_j\) is a constant and factors out of the inner expectation, which EGLP makes zero. Every past-reward term vanishes, leaving \(\hat R_t\). Future rewards depend on \(a_t\) (through the states it leads to), so they don't factor out of the inner expectation — the argument fails, as it must, or the whole gradient would be zero.

2.6 REINFORCE: the first runnable algorithm

REINFORCE (Williams, 1992)
  loop:
    collect N trajectories with current π_θ
    compute reward-to-go R̂_t for every (s_t, a_t)
    ĝ = (1/N) Σ_traj Σ_t ∇_θ log π_θ(a_t|s_t) · R̂_t
    θ ← θ + α ĝ          # ONE step, then discard ALL data
The ancestor. Everything PPO adds — baseline, GAE, ratio, clip, epochs — is bolted onto this skeleton.

In a deep-learning framework you implement \(\hat g\) by building the pseudo-loss \(L(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{\tau}\sum_t \log\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)\, \hat R_t\) (with \(\hat R_t\) treated as a constant — detached!) and calling autograd: \(\nabla_\theta L = \hat g\). Two standing warnings:

The pseudo-loss is not a loss

Its value is meaningless as a performance measure — only its gradient at the current \(\theta\) equals the policy gradient. It can go up while the policy gets worse and vice versa. Monitor average return, never the pseudo-loss. And if you forget to detach \(\hat R_t\) when it is produced by a learned value function (Chapter 3), gradients leak into the critic through the wrong path — a classic silent bug (§7.5).

2.7 Why REINFORCE isn't enough

Two compounding problems:

  • Variance. The estimator weights whole log-prob sums by noisy scalar returns. Returns vary across trajectories due to stochasticity in \(\rho_0\), \(P\), and \(\pi\) itself; variance grows roughly with horizon, and gradient signal-to-noise can be abysmal. Small batches give erratic steps; usable batches get huge. Chapter 3 attacks this with baselines and GAE.
  • One step per batch. After a single parameter update the data is off-policy and the estimator is biased for the new \(\theta\), so REINFORCE must discard it. Thousands of environment steps buy one gradient step. Chapters 4–6 build the machinery (importance ratios + a trust region) that lets PPO take many steps per batch safely.

Write REINFORCE in pseudocode from memory (collection, estimator, update, data handling). Then name its two structural weaknesses and, for each, the chapter-3-to-6 mechanism that addresses it.

What must happen to the batch after one gradient step, and why?

Collect trajectories under \(\pi_\theta\); compute \(\hat R_t\); \(\hat g = \frac{1}{N}\sum_\tau\sum_t \nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)\hat R_t\); one ascent step; discard data (it's now off-policy). Weakness 1: high variance of the Monte-Carlo return weights → fixed by baselines/advantages and GAE (Ch 3). Weakness 2: one update per batch → fixed by the importance-sampled surrogate (Ch 4) made safe by a trust region (Ch 5) or clipping (Ch 6), which allow several epochs per batch.

You implement REINFORCE with the pseudo-loss \(L = \frac{1}{N}\sum \log\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)\hat R_t\). (a) Why must \(\hat R_t\) be detached from the autograd graph? (b) Why is the value of \(L\) useless for monitoring training? (c) What should you monitor instead?

What is the only property of \(L\) that the theory guarantees?

(a) The theory says the gradient flows only through \(\log\pi_\theta\); if \(\hat R_t\) carries gradients (e.g. it contains a critic's output), autograd adds spurious terms that are not part of the policy gradient. (b) Only \(\nabla L\) at the current parameters is meaningful; \(L\)'s value is an arbitrary weighted log-likelihood, not expected return — it routinely moves opposite to performance. (c) Average episodic return (and later: explained variance, KL, clip fraction — §7.4).
