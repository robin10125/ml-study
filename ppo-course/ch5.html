Chapter 5 · TRPO: Trust Region Policy Optimization

Chapter 4 left us with a local model \(L\) and a question: how far can we trust it? TRPO (Schulman et al., 2015) answers with a theorem — improvement is guaranteed if the step is small in KL — and an algorithm: maximize \(L\) inside a hard KL ball, solved with natural gradients, conjugate gradient, and a line search. PPO exists because this machinery works but is heavy; you must understand what it does to appreciate what PPO replaces it with.

5.1 The monotonic improvement theorem

How wrong can the surrogate be? TRPO's theoretical core bounds the error by the policy divergence:

TRPO lower bound
$$ \eta(\pi') \;\ge\; \eta(\pi) + L_\pi(\pi') \;-\; C\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\max}(\pi, \pi'), \qquad C = \frac{4\, \epsilon\, \gamma}{(1-\gamma)^2}, $$

where \(\epsilon = \max_{s,a}|A^\pi(s,a)|\) and \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\max}\) is the KL maximized over states. The surrogate, debited by a KL penalty, under-promises: the true objective is at least the penalized surrogate.

Why this guarantees monotonic improvement: define \(M(\pi') = \eta(\pi) + L_\pi(\pi') - C\,D^{\max}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi,\pi')\). Then (i) \(\eta(\pi') \ge M(\pi')\) for all \(\pi'\), and (ii) \(M(\pi) = \eta(\pi)\) (at \(\pi'=\pi\), both \(L\) and KL are zero — §4.4). So if you choose \(\pi'\) maximizing \(M\), then

$$ \eta(\pi') \ge M(\pi') \ge M(\pi) = \eta(\pi). $$

Each iteration can only help. This is minorize–maximize (MM): \(M\) is a global lower bound (minorant) touching \(\eta\) at the current point — the same template as EM. The intuition behind the bound itself: the only lie in \(L\) is the frozen state distribution; if the policies are close in KL at every state, their trajectory distributions are close, so the visitation distributions \(d^{\pi}\) and \(d^{\pi'}\) cannot differ much, and the lie is bounded — with the horizon factor \(1/(1-\gamma)^2\) pricing how long the distribution shift has to compound.

Property (i): minorant. Property (ii): tangency at the current policy — uses \(L_\pi(\pi)=0\) and \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi,\pi)=0\).

\(\eta(\pi') \ge \eta(\pi) + L_\pi(\pi') - C D^{\max}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi,\pi')\) with \(C = 4\epsilon\gamma/(1-\gamma)^2\), \(\epsilon = \max|A^\pi|\). Let \(M(\pi') = \eta(\pi) + L_\pi(\pi') - C D^{\max}_{\mathrm{KL}}\). For the maximizer \(\pi^+\): \(\eta(\pi^+) \ge M(\pi^+)\) [bound, property (i)] \(\ge M(\pi)\) [\(\pi^+\) maximizes \(M\)] \(= \eta(\pi)\) [tangency, property (ii): both penalty terms vanish at \(\pi'=\pi\)]. Hence monotonic improvement.

5.2 From penalty to constraint

Using the theoretical penalty directly is hopeless: \(C\) involves \(\max_{s,a}|A^\pi|\) and \(1/(1-\gamma)^2\) — with \(\gamma = 0.99\) that's a factor of \(4\times10^4\,\epsilon\), prescribing microscopically conservative steps. TRPO makes two pragmatic substitutions: trade the penalty for a hard constraint with a tunable radius \(\delta\), and replace the unwieldy \(\max_s\) KL with the average KL over visited states:

The TRPO optimization problem
$$ \max_\theta\; \mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi_\text{old}},\, a\sim\pi_\text{old}}\Big[ \frac{\pi_\theta(a|s)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(a|s)} \hat A(s,a) \Big] \quad \text{s.t.}\quad \bar D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta_\text{old}, \theta) := \mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi_\text{old}}}\big[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot|s) \,\|\, \pi_\theta(\cdot|s)) \big] \le \delta. $$

Typical \(\delta \approx 0.01\). The constraint form is preferred over a fixed penalty coefficient because no single coefficient works across problems or even across iterations of one problem — a hard radius is far easier to set than a price. (Remember this sentence: it is also why PPO's adaptive-KL variant exists, §6.5.)

5.3 Solving it: linearize the objective, quadratize the constraint

For a small step \(\Delta = \theta - \theta_\text{old}\), expand both pieces around \(\theta_\text{old}\): the objective to first order (its value and gradient \(g = \nabla_\theta L|_{\theta_\text{old}}\) — which by §4.4 is the policy gradient), the constraint to second order (its value and gradient vanish at \(\Delta=0\), §4.5, leaving the Fisher quadratic):

$$ \max_\Delta\; g^\top \Delta \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta^\top F \Delta \le \delta. $$

Lagrangian \(g^\top\Delta - \frac{\mu}{2}\Delta^\top F\Delta\); stationarity gives \(g = \mu F \Delta\), so the direction is \(\Delta \propto F^{-1} g\) — the natural gradient. The constraint is active at the optimum (the objective is linear — push to the boundary), which pins the length: setting \(\Delta = \beta F^{-1}g\) in \(\frac12 \Delta^\top F\Delta = \delta\) yields \(\frac{\beta^2}{2} g^\top F^{-1} g = \delta\), hence

The TRPO step
$$ \Delta^{*} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{\,g^\top F^{-1} g\,}}\; F^{-1} g. $$

\(F^{-1}\) re-expresses the gradient in behavior space: directions in which the policy distribution is hyper-sensitive (large Fisher eigenvalue) get shrunk, insensitive directions get amplified. The resulting step of behavioral size \(\sqrt{2\delta}\) is the same policy change regardless of how the network is parametrized — the §4.1 problem, solved exactly to second order.

Why does \(F^{-1}g\) fix the parametrization-sensitivity problem of §4.1?

Stationarity: \(g = \mu F\Delta\). Then substitute \(\Delta = \beta F^{-1}g\) into \(\frac12\Delta^\top F\Delta = \delta\).

\(L\)'s zeroth/first-order data at \(\theta_\text{old}\) are 0 and \(g\) (the policy gradient); KL's zeroth/first orders vanish, so its leading term is \(\frac12\Delta^\top F\Delta\). Problem: \(\max g^\top\Delta\) s.t. \(\frac12\Delta^\top F\Delta\le\delta\). Lagrangian stationarity: \(g = \mu F\Delta \Rightarrow \Delta \propto F^{-1}g\). A linear objective increases along the ray, so the optimum sits on the boundary; \(\frac{\beta^2}{2}g^\top F^{-1}g = \delta \Rightarrow \beta = \sqrt{2\delta/(g^\top F^{-1}g)}\). \(F\) measures how strongly each parameter direction moves the distribution; preconditioning by \(F^{-1}\) equalizes this, making the step a fixed-size move in behavior space, invariant to reparametrization.

5.4 Conjugate gradient: \(F^{-1}g\) without ever forming \(F\)

For a network with \(n\) parameters (easily \(10^5\)–\(10^7\)), \(F\) is \(n\times n\): storing it is impossible, inverting it absurd. TRPO's engineering insight: you never need \(F\) — only the ability to compute Fisher–vector products \(v \mapsto Fv\). Two facts combine:

  • Conjugate gradient solves \(Fx = g\) for symmetric positive-definite \(F\) using only matvecs — and ~10 iterations give an \(x \approx F^{-1}g\) good enough for a trust-region step (we don't need an exact solve; the line search mops up).
  • \(Fv\) via double backprop: \(F\) is the Hessian of the average KL at \(\theta_\text{old}\) (§4.5). So compute the scalar \(\bar D_{\mathrm{KL}}\), backprop to get \(\nabla\bar D_{\mathrm{KL}}\), dot with the fixed vector \(v\), and backprop the scalar \(\nabla\bar D_{\mathrm{KL}}^\top v\) again: the result is \(\nabla^2\bar D_{\mathrm{KL}}\, v = Fv\). Cost: a small constant times one gradient evaluation. (In practice a damped product \((F + \beta I)v\), \(\beta\sim 0.1\), keeps CG stable.)

(c) starts from a scalar: the average KL between the frozen old policy and the current one, evaluated at \(\theta_\text{old}\).

(a) \(F\) is \(n\times n\) with \(n\) up to millions of parameters — \(O(n^2)\) memory to store, \(O(n^3)\) to invert; both impossible. (b) CG solves SPD systems using only products \(v\mapsto Fv\), never the matrix, and converges fast in the large eigen-directions — ~10 iterations suffice for an approximate trust-region direction. (c) Compute scalar \(k = \bar D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta_\text{old}\|\theta)\) at \(\theta = \theta_\text{old}\); autograd → \(u = \nabla_\theta k\) (a vector, kept in the graph); form scalar \(u^\top v\) with \(v\) detached; autograd again → \(\nabla_\theta(u^\top v) = \nabla^2 k\, v = Fv\), since the KL Hessian at the expansion point is the Fisher matrix.

5.5 The line search: trust, but verify

The step \(\Delta^*\) was derived from approximations — linearized objective, quadratic KL. The true KL at \(\theta_\text{old} + \Delta^*\) may exceed \(\delta\), and the true surrogate may even decrease. TRPO therefore backtracks: try \(\theta_\text{old} + \alpha^j \Delta^*\) for \(\alpha \approx 0.8\), \(j = 0, 1, 2, \dots\), accepting the first candidate that satisfies both checks, now computed exactly (no Taylor expansion):

  1. the actual average KL constraint: \(\bar D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta_\text{old}, \theta) \le \delta\);
  2. actual surrogate improvement: \(\hat L(\theta) \gt \hat L(\theta_\text{old})\) (with a sufficient-decrease flavor in practice).

If ten halvings all fail, take no step. This final verification — checking the real quantities, not the model — is what makes TRPO so reliable in practice: a bad quadratic model costs you a smaller step, never a destroyed policy.

The bill for the guarantee:

  • Complexity — CG loop, double backprop, KL evaluations, line search: several hundred lines of delicate code versus a dozen for the eventual PPO loss.
  • Compute — each update costs ~10 Fisher-vector products plus line-search evaluations, several times a plain gradient step.
  • Rigidity — the clean \(F^{-1}g\) story strains with architectures whose policy and value share parameters (the constrained problem no longer factors cleanly), with very large networks (CG cost), and it can't exploit minibatch optimizers: each batch yields one carefully verified step, where PPO will take dozens of cheap ones.

Hold the question "what did the constraint actually buy?" in mind: a limit on how far the new policy's action distribution drifts from the data-collecting policy's. Chapter 6 buys approximately the same thing with a clamp.
